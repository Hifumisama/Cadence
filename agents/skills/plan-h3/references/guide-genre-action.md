# Genre "action" — writing a physical gesture or a clash

This scene is action: what the body does must be **readable**. A spectacular effect with no gesture producing it gives a frozen character in front of a moving set.

1. **Whole body first.** Describe the footing (feet, hips, shoulders), then the limb that strikes or guides. A readable chain: **preparation → impulse → effect → recovery**. "He bends his knees, his weight shifts to the front leg, the arm shoots straight, the impact lifts the dust" turns into an image; "he masters the element" does not.
2. **Use the brief's gesture.** If the character has a `gestuelle` (fighting style, trade, gait), translate it into precise **postures and movements** (low stances and grounded strikes, spins and dodges, or the heavy gait of a craftsman at work…). "A dance", "graceful movements" are empty words: the model renders them as hand gestures.
3. **The wide shot shows the gesture; the detail comes after.** A close-up on a lone hand, without the body that threw it, makes the gesture tiny. Open on a frame showing the body and the effect, then cut to the detail.
4. **The effect follows the gesture, never precedes it.** Matter (water, stone, flame, cloth, dust) reacts **after** the impulse and continues its direction. Describe its matter, direction and speed.
5. **Describe the mechanics of each move; its name alone buys nothing.** A foreign or technical name ("mawashi geri", "ashi barai") is rendered only if it resonates with something the model knows, and then the description does the work anyway (render bench, 18 clips). Write each blow as one clause with four parts: **who**, **which limb or weapon** and its path (footing, pivot, arc: "she pivots on her standing foot and her rear leg whips round in a flat arc to the head"), **what it aims at or touches** (a sweep must name the ankles it meets), **what visibly happens** (sparks, dust, a step back, cloth snapping). "A high slash is swept aside by the forearm in a spray of sparks" is an action; "a frantic, chaotic clash" is not. Fix the number of readable contacts per shot (two to four) and simplify the choreography around each impact: fast hand or blade contacts distort when too many pile up in under two seconds. **Chain the moves logically**: the receiver's reaction is the physical consequence of that very blow (a sweep at the ankles takes his legs from under him and he falls; a palm to the sternum throws him back), and the next blow starts from where the last one left both fighters. A reaction that does not fit the blow (a forearm block against a low sweep) confuses the model.
6. **Both fighters are in the frame at the contact.** The next shot picks the blow up mid-motion. A flying weapon or body is followed by the camera and ends somewhere visible (the dagger thunks into a carved post).
7. **A blow has weight, and a sound.** At the contact, describe what moves from the outside in, with a verb proper to the material (cloth snaps, stone cracks, dust rings off the floor); "impacts", "strikes" or "slices through" alone are not enough. The contact is the strongest event of the shot and is heard at that very moment (a dull thud, a crack of stone), written in the text of that shot; only the background sound (distant clangs, crowd, wind) goes in `overall_soundscape`. Show what the contact does: a ring of dust off the floor, a wave through the cloth, a cracked plank, a lantern swinging, the camera shaking. The decisive blow may hold for a heartbeat: "the frame slows almost to stillness, dust and embers hanging in the air, then time snaps back", with a close-up on the face receiving it.
8. **Hold a breath before the finale.** One beat of stillness (embers rising around the motionless character, only footsteps heard) makes the big effect land.
9. **The big effect acts on the set.** Say what breaks and in what order (beams tear away, screens fold outward off their frames, floorboards buckle in a wave, lanterns pop), what light it throws, and how it moves the other character's clothing and hair. An effect that touches nothing reads as a bonfire.
10. **The character sheet stands alone**: effects and props are said in prose (or come from an effect asset), not from the character's image.
